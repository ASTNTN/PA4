PA3 Report: Blocking the Outer Product

Name: [your name]  ·  Date: 2026-10-18  ·  Machine: eel (i9-13900K, E-cores only, Turbo off)

Starting Point

The kernel is OPQ's c[i+j] += a[i] * b[j] over the whole (i, j) rectangle, now cut into tiles. Part A makes one tile run as fast as possible on one core; Part B spreads the tiles over the 16 E-cores. All headline timing is at 100,000 × 100,000 (1010 multiply-adds, 2·1010 flops). Peak (TPP) is 35.2 GFLOP/s per E-core, 563.2 GFLOP/s for all 16. Sequential runs are pinned to core 16; parallel runs use _gate.sh's binding (cores 16–31, OMP_PROC_BIND=close, cluster-cyclic places: the first four threads land on four different clusters). Every variant passed the shared gate (42 oracle cases and 14 gold cases, sequential and parallel checking builds) before it was timed, except the one deliberately racy diagnostic in Experiment 11. Timings are medians of 5 or 7 reps from sweep.sh; hardware counters are from perf stat on the cpu_atom (E-core) events, normalized per useful vector FMA.

Background: what an FMA is, and why everything is counted per FMA

Each (i, j) pair is one multiply and one add. A fused multiply-add (FMA) instruction does both, and the 256-bit version does 8 of them side by side: one vector FMA is 16 flops. An E-core can complete one vector FMA per cycle; at 2.2 GHz that is 35.2 GFLOP/s, the TPP used throughout.

So "cycles per vector FMA" is a direct yardstick: 1.0 is peak, 1.4 is 71% of peak. Other counters are divided the same way (one 100K × 100K rep is 100,0012 / 8 ≈ 1.25·109 vector FMAs).

Background: tiles, and the 4-loop structure

Blocking cuts the (i, j) rectangle into bs × bs tiles and finishes one tile before starting the next: two loops (ii, jj) walk the tiles and two (i, j) walk inside one. Tile (I, J) reads bs values of a and of b and adds into a 2·bs − 1 long stretch of c, so its working set is fixed by bs, not by the problem size. Tiles at the right and bottom edges are left incomplete (shorter loops), never padded with extra work.

Experiment 1: plain blocking (BKQ)sequential

Question

Does tiling OPQ's two loops, with nothing else changed, change the single-core rate, and how does it depend on the block size?

Hypothesis

At PA2's small sizes BKQ matches OPQ (~37% TPP): the inner loop, and so its 2 loads and 1 store per vector FMA, is unchanged. At large sizes, where OPQ's rows outgrow L1 and it falls to ~27%, blocking should hold the small-size plateau.

Experiment Design

Square tiles, 4 loops, edge tiles incomplete, runtime knob --tune block_size (64 when absent). The inner j loop vectorizes 8-wide exactly as OPQ's (vbroadcastss of a[i], vfmadd213ps with c as a memory operand, one store). Block sweeps: 16–1,024 at 1,000 × 4,000 and 8,000 × 2,000 (5 reps), then 256–4,096 at 20K, 40K, 60K, 80K and 100K square (7 reps).

Experiment 2: unroll-and-jam, R = 8 (BKQ2)sequential

Question

The limit is loads per FMA. Unroll-and-jam (trick 1 of the PA2 reference report) loads and stores each vector of c once per 8 FMAs instead of once per FMA. How much of that shows up inside a tile?

Hypothesis

Loads per FMA fall from 2 to 9/8 and BKQ2 rises to ~55–60% TPP. The assembly showed that -ffast-math regroups the 8 products into pairs (4 multiplies + 4 FMAs + 4 adds, 12 FP instructions for 8 FMAs of work), which caps the loop at 8/12 = 67%.

Background: unroll-and-jam on the outer product

Take 8 rows i0 .. i0+7 of a tile together. Row r adds a[i0+r] · b[j] into c[i0+r+j]. Renaming t = r + j, all 8 rows add into the same c[i0+t], row r using b[t − r]. So one fused loop over t loads c[i0+t] once, does 8 FMAs with 8 values of a held in registers and 8 windows of b one float apart, and stores once: 9 loads and 1 store per 8 FMAs instead of 16 and 8.

The fused loop only covers the t where every row has its j inside the tile (t = jj+7 .. jend−1). The leftover triangles at each end of each row, the "ragged ends", ran as plain scalar OPQ rows. They matter a lot in Experiments 7 and 8.

Experiment Design

BKQ's tiling, R = 8 fixed, 8 values of a hoisted into locals, ragged ends and leftover rows as plain rows. Same large-size block sweeps as Experiment 1 (BKQ re-swept alongside for a direct comparison).

Experiment 3: keep the chain of 8 FMAs (BKQ3)sequential

Question

Does removing the 12-instruction regrouping (trick 2) lift the kernel toward the 67% cap and past it?

Hypothesis

With __attribute__((optimize("no-associative-math"))) on the kernel only, the loop becomes one load of b, 8 chained FMAs and one store. BKQ3 beats BKQ2 and levels off at 60–67%, where the chain of 8 dependent FMAs (6-cycle latency) becomes the next limit.

Experiment Design

BKQ2 plus the attribute; the rest of the build keeps -ffast-math. Same block sweeps. The hot loop was checked in the disassembly:

vmovups     (%r14,%rax,1),%ymm0         ; b window, row 0
vfmadd213ps (%rdx,%rax,1),%ymm15,%ymm0  ; * a0, + c
vfmadd231ps (%r11,%rax,1),%ymm14,%ymm0  ; + a1 * b window, row 1
...                                      ; 5 more
vfmadd231ps (%rbx,%rax,1),%ymm8,%ymm0   ; + a7 * b window, row 7
vmovups     %ymm0,(%rdx,%rax,1)         ; store c

BKQ3 is 4–6% faster than BKQ2 at every block size and problem size; best 59.1% (20.8 GFLOP/s) at 3,072–4,096. That is far less than the ~16% this step gave in the PA2 reference report, and BKQ2 was already well under its cap, so regrouping was not the main limit.

Background: split loads

Memory moves in 64-byte cache lines. A 32-byte load that starts in the last 31 bytes of a line straddles two lines and costs the load port extra time. The 8 rows read b windows starting one float apart, so in any one copy of b most of them can't start on an aligned address, and some cross a line.

Conclusions

Partly supported: the chain helps by ~5%, but the next limit is not latency (the FP scheduler is never full). BKQ3 is load-side bound: the load buffer is full 28% of cycles and one load µop in five is a split. That points to aligned copies of b (trick 4) rather than interleaved accumulators.

Experiment 4: four aligned, shifted copies of b (BKQ4)sequential

Question

Does reading b from aligned copies remove the split loads, and what does that buy?

Hypothesis

Split loads of b fall to near zero (only the unaligned loads of c, 1 per 8 FMAs, may still split) and BKQ4 rises above 59%. The copies add footprint, so the best block may move down.

Experiment Design

Once per call, inside the timed kernel, build 4 copies of b on 64-byte-aligned bases, copy s shifted right by s floats (bc[s][x+s] = b[x]). For each tile, row r reads from the copy that puts all its loads on 16-byte boundaries, which never cross a line. Only the fused loop uses the copies. Same block sweeps.

Experiment 5: where the copies sit (BKQ4, copy spacing)sequential

Question

Are the extra misses at 100K a capacity problem (the tile no longer fits) or a placement problem (the copies collide in the same L1 sets)?

Hypothesis

Placement. Moving the copies apart with padding brings some spacings back to ~61–62% TPP and leaves others near 55%. If the misses were capacity misses, padding wouldn't change them.

Background: L1 sets, and why 4,096 bytes

The E-core's L1 data cache is 32 KB, 8-way, 64 sets of 64-byte lines (read from /sys/devices/system/cpu/cpu16/cache/index0; Procinfo1's 48 KB appears to be the P-core figure). An address's set is fixed by its bits 6–11, so two addresses 4,096 bytes apart always land in the same set. Each set holds at most 8 lines; streams that keep landing in the same sets evict each other although the cache as a whole has room. Separately, a load can be delayed if its address matches an older pending store in its low 12 bits ("4K aliasing").

Experiment Design

Runtime knob --tune copy_pad=N: N floats (rounded up to 16) between consecutive copies; only addresses change. 64 pads one line apart cover every spacing mod 4,096 bytes. Swept at 100K, 80K and 40K, bs = 3,072, 5 reps; one perf run per pad at 0, 16, 336 and 864 (100K).

Experiment Run

03-BKQ4/results/2026.10.18.00{6,7,8}_copy_pad_sweep_*.csv. Reps within 2%.

Analysis

@@C_SPACING@@
  • The rate depends on the copy spacing mod 4,096 alone: at every spacing the three sizes agree within ~0.3 points. Most spacings give 61–62%; the default at 100K (2,752) happens to be a bad one, which is all the 100K drop of Experiment 4 was.
  • Bad spacings sit just next to, not at, multiples of 4,096 and of 2,048 and 4,096/3. Read off the table: a spacing S is bad when S, 2S or 3S mod 4,096 (the distances between pairs of copies) lands 64–192 bytes from a multiple of 4,096 without being one. The worst spacings cost 28% (45.6% vs 63.1%).
  • Exactly 0 mod 4,096 is the best spacing at every size: 63.1% (22.2 GFLOP/s).
  • ld_blocks.address_alias equals the L1-miss count within 2–10% at all four profiled pads, and both vary 8× with padding. From these counters I can't tell L1 set conflicts from 4K aliasing against the stores to c.

Conclusions

Supported: the misses are a placement effect, not capacity. From here on (all BKP variants and BKQ5–7) the copy spacing is rounded up to a multiple of 4,096 bytes. The mechanism stays open.

Experiment 6: unrolling the fused loop (BKQ5)sequential

Question

Once the aligned copies removed the split loads, BKQ4's profile showed the FP scheduler full ~30% of slots. Each output vector is a chain of 8 dependent FMAs (≈ 48 cycles). Does putting more independent chains next to each other help?

Hypothesis

#pragma GCC unroll 4 on the fused loop gives 4 independent chains per pass, the FP scheduler share falls, and the rate rises above ~63%. Interleaving the chains instruction by instruction would need intrinsics, which BPM_ACC rules out.

Experiment Design

BKQ4 with the 4,096-byte spacing plus the pragma. In the disassembly the unroll works, but the 4 chains are emitted one after another, not interleaved. GCC's pre-allocation scheduler (optimize("schedule-insns"), tried on a scratch copy) interleaved two chains at a time but added a vmovaps register copy for nearly every FMA, so it was not used. Quick check: 100K, core 16, 3 reps, twice; control = the same source without the pragma.

Experiment 7: rectangular tiles (BKQ6)sequential

Question

BKQ4 still missed L1 on ~10% of loads even at a good placement. A tile that is tall in i and narrow in j touches a narrower window of the copies of b. Does any width below the square tile do better?

Hypothesis

With the height fixed, the rate first rises with width (the scalar ragged ends are 56 of 8w products per group of 8 rows) and, if the copy window's L1 misses matter, peaks below the square tile.

Experiment Design

New knob block_width (default = block_size); everything else is the BKQ4 kernel with 4,096-byte spacing. Widths 1 to n at heights n = 3,072 and 1,024, 100K, 3 reps.

Deviation. The shared gate tune (BPM_GATE_TUNE in shared/include/kernel.h) has no block_width entry, so the gate only runs square 64 × 64 tiles. The header says to add every new key, but it is shared infrastructure and was not changed. Instead, scratch checking builds with block_width = 1, 5, 8, 24, 40, 64 added to the gate tune were run; all passed. The same was done for BKQ7.

  • A cost model fits the points from w = 32 up within ~4%: time per group of 8 rows = x · (vector iterations) + y · (scalar products: 56 ragged-end products plus 8 per leftover fused-loop iteration) + z. Refuted: narrow tiles don't help. The narrow-width loss is the fixed scalar edge work of each row group, and by the model it is still ~10% of the time of a square 1,024 tile. That made it the next target.

Experiment 8: doing the edges in the vector loop (BKQ7)sequential

Question

Can every product of a full row group go through the vectorized fused loop, and what does that buy?

Hypothesis

Up to ~10% at bs = 1,024 (the model's scalar share), most of the narrow-tile penalty gone, at the cost of up to 2 extra vector iterations of zero products per group.

Experiment Design

The copies of b become one zero-padded segment per tile column: in copy s, segment J holds the tile's w values of b after 7 zeros, then zeros. Row r reads through p[r] = segment + s + 7 − r, so p[r][u] = b[jj + u − r] inside the tile and 0 outside it. The fused loop then runs over the whole trapezoid of the group (u = 0 .. w+6), rounded up to a multiple of 8 so the vector loop has no remainder. Where a row has no b in the tile it adds a · 0, which leaves c unchanged for finite inputs. Every segment starts on a 64-byte boundary, so the copy for row r is simply (r + 1) mod 4. Only the leftover rows of the bottom tile row (height not a multiple of 8) stay scalar. The hot loop is unchanged: one load of b, 8 chained FMAs, one store.

Experiment Run

Block sweep 256–4,096 at 100K, 7 reps (03-BKQ7/results/2026.10.18.002_bs_sweep_100000x100000.csv); then every single-core variant re-timed at its best block (*_best_bs*_100000x100000.csv in each directory). Reps within 0.2%.

Experiment 9: tile rows with an array reduction (BKP1)parallel

Question

How far does the simplest race-free split go: whole tile rows dealt out statically, each thread adding into its own copy of c?

Hypothesis

Near-linear, limited by load imbalance. At 100K there are 98 / 66 / 33 tile rows for bs = 1,024 / 1,536 / 3,072; at 16 threads the busiest thread has 7 / 5 / 3 rows against an average of 6.1 / 4.1 / 2.1, capping speedup at ~14.0× / ~13.2× / ~11×.

Background: array reductions

reduction(+:c[:n]) gives each thread a private, zeroed copy of all of c; threads add into their own copy, and OpenMP sums the copies into c at the end. No two threads ever write the same memory, so any split of the work is race-free, at the cost of zeroing and merging t copies (here 800 KB each).

Experiment Design

BKQ4 with 4,096-byte spacing; #pragma omp parallel for schedule(static) reduction(+:c[:n]) on the ii loop (the checking build reduces the Kahan array comp the same way). Copies of b built before the parallel region and shared. Threads 1–16 at bs = 1,024, 1,536, 3,072, 5 reps.

Experiment 11: the checkerboard, and which tiles really race (BKP3)paralleldiagnostic

Question

PA3.md's second approach drops the reduction: "a tile has a race only with its immediate four neighbors", so a checkerboard of tiles, run one colour at a time, should be race-free. Is it, for this tiling, and what does dropping the private copies buy?

Hypothesis

Checked first by brute force (bs = 4, 5 × 5 tiles): tile (I, J) writes c[(I+J)·bs .. (I+J+2)·bs − 2], so two tiles overlap exactly when their I + J differ by 0 or 1. All tiles on the same anti-diagonal write the same range of c, and they have the same checkerboard colour, so the literal checkerboard races (30 same-colour overlapping pairs in the 5 × 5 example). The parallel checking build should fail; the sequential one should pass.

@@D_OVERLAP@@

That does not match PA3.md's "four neighbors" statement for this tiling (perhaps the lecture assumed a different index space; not resolved). It was built anyway, as a diagnostic, to see the race and its cost.

Experiment Design

BKP2 without the reduction: two phases (colour 0, then 1), each a parallel for collapse(2) over the tile grid that skips the other colour; the implicit barrier separates the phases; threads add straight into the shared c. Timed with sweep.sh --allow-gate-failure (results under results/diagnostic/).

Experiment Run

Gate, twice: sequential build passed; parallel build failed 11–12 of 42 oracle and 4 of 14 gold cases, every failing case with several tile rows (e.g. 20K × 20K random: ~31,000 of 40,001 outputs wrong, worst error 3–4·104 × the tolerance).

The first timing run was half of BKP2 already at one thread, where nothing can race (10.7 vs 20.8 GFLOP/s at bs = 1,024). The non-OpenMP build of the same source ran at full speed. -fopt-info-vec reported the fused loop "versioned for vectorization because of possible aliasing", and perf annotate showed a store of c after every FMA, 8 per 8 FMAs instead of one.

Background: why OpenMP lost the restrict guarantee

GCC compiles the body of a parallel for into a separate ("outlined") function and passes the shared variables to it through a structure. Inside that function c is a plain pointer: the restrict on kernel_multiply's parameter doesn't come along. The compiler must then assume a store to c could change the b values the next FMA reads, so it can't keep c[i0+t] in a register across the 8 rows. In BKP2 each thread's private reduction copy is a local array, provably separate from b, so the problem never showed.

Fix: the body of one tile moved into bkq_tile, a noinline function whose pointer parameters are all restrict-qualified. One store per 8 FMAs again, no aliasing versioning, 1-thread speed back to 20.7 GFLOP/s. The diagnostic timing was re-run on the fixed code (bs = 1,024 and 3,072, 5 reps; 03-BKP3/results/diagnostic/2026.10.18.00{3,4}_*).

Experiment 12: diagonal parity, race-free without a reduction (BKP4)parallel

Question

Tiles on diagonals d and d + 2 never write the same element. Running whole diagonals of one parity at a time is race-free with no private copies. Is it faster than the reduction?

Hypothesis

It passes the gate, matches BKP2 at one thread and matches or beats it at 16, since it skips zeroing and merging the private copies. Load imbalance should be small: a greedy simulation of dynamic scheduling in diagonal order (unit tile cost, edges ignored) gives 99.5% / 99.0% / 98.6% efficiency at 16 threads for bs = 1,024 / 1,536 / 3,072.

Experiment 13: the final kernel on 16 cores (BKP5)parallel

Question

Does BKQ7's single-core gain carry into BKP4's schedule, and which block size is best once single-core speed no longer depends on it?

Hypothesis

BKP5 keeps BKP4's near-linear scaling at BKQ7's per-core rate: ~22% over BKP4, ~400 GFLOP/s at 16 threads. The best block is set by the parallel side, probably 768–1,536.

Experiment Design

bkq_tile becomes BKQ7's tile body (per-tile-column zero-padded segments, fused loop over the whole trapezoid), in BKP4's diagonal-parity schedule.

A race BKQ7 didn't have. BKQ7 rounds each group's loop up to a multiple of 8 with zero products, which writes up to 6 elements past the top of the tile's range of c. Adding 0 is still a read-modify-write, and those elements belong to diagonal d + 2, which runs at the same time. BKP5 keeps the padding inside [ii + jj − 1, iend + jend − 2]: above the group's range where there is room (every group but a tile's last), otherwise one element below (diagonal d − 2 ends two below). Besides the gate, the write ranges were checked by enumeration with the same formulas, for 308 cases (gate sizes, 100K, edge shapes, 300 random shapes and block sizes): every diagonal's range ends below the start of diagonal d + 2.

Experiment Run

Gate passed. Threads 1–16 × blocks 256–4,096, 5 reps (03-BKP5/results/2026.10.18.002_threads_x_bs_100000x100000.csv), then a plain thread sweep at the best block (.003). One thread: 25.1 GFLOP/s, ~1% under BKQ7.

Final configurations

  • BKQ7 (Part A): square tiles, R = 8 unroll-and-jam with the chain of 8 FMAs kept (no-associative-math), four shifted copies of b built as zero-padded 64-byte-aligned segments per tile column, spaced a multiple of 4,096 bytes, fused loop over each row group's whole trapezoid. 72.2% TPP on one E-core at bs = 768, 70–72% at any block from 256 to 4,096.
  • BKP5 (Part B): BKQ7's tile body in a noinline, restrict-qualified function; tiles grouped by anti-diagonal, even diagonals then odd, whole diagonals dealt with schedule(dynamic, 1); zero padding kept inside each tile's own range of c. No reduction. 397 GFLOP/s at 16 threads, bs = 512 (512–1,024 equivalent).

The most useful things learned

  1. Each single-core step removed one limit and exposed the next: loads per FMA (unroll-and-jam), compiler regrouping (keep the chain), split loads (aligned copies), placement of the copies, scalar edge work, and now the FMA chain itself. Measuring before each step (the BKQ3 profile pointed at splits, not latency) avoided working on the wrong one.
  2. Where the copies of b sit relative to each other mod 4,096 bytes changed the rate by up to 1.4× at the same block size; a spacing that is an exact multiple of 4,096 was best at every size.
  3. The fixed scalar edge work per row group cost far more than its share of the products suggested. Doing it inside the vector loop, with zero-padded copies, gave +22% and made the block size irrelevant on one core.
  4. For (i, j) tiles of this kernel, every tile on an anti-diagonal writes the same range of c: the races are along diagonals, not just with four neighbours. Diagonals of one parity are mutually race-free, and that schedule beat the array reduction by 3–7%.
  5. Writes of zero are still writes: padding that never changes a value can still race if another thread owns the element.
  6. OpenMP's outlining drops restrict on shared pointers, which halved the kernel in BKP3. Putting the tile body in a restrict-qualified function fixed it; the disassembly check (one store of c per 8 FMAs) caught it.

Open items

  • The kernel is now bound by the FMA chains (FP scheduler full 48%). The PA2 reference report used U = 3 interleaved accumulators for this; BKQ5's #pragma GCC unroll did not interleave and was 14–22% slower at large blocks, unexplained. Shorter chains (two partial sums per output) would change the summation order and how BPM_ACC is applied.
  • Past ~85% of TPP the load port is the limit: fewer loads per FMA would need b vectors reused across rows within 16 registers.
  • Unexplained: why BKQ7 gained twice the model's estimate; the ~2% dip at bs = 1,536–2,048 in BKQ7; w = 4 slower than w = 2; whether the placement effect is L1 sets or 4K aliasing; why BKP2's L1 misses per FMA double with threads; bs = 4,096's drop at 13 threads in BKP5.
  • PA3.md's "four neighbors" race statement does not match this tiling; worth asking the instructor which index space it meant.
  • The shared gate tune has no block_width; rectangular tiles were only checked with scratch builds. Adding it needs an approved change to shared/include/kernel.h.
  • The copies of b are built serially inside the timed kernel; their cost at 16 threads was not measured.

Notes on method

  • sweep.sh --record-seq refuses --tune, so each recorded T(seq) is the untuned default (bs = 64). Thread-sweep speedups are against each sweep's own 1-thread time (min of the two, as the harness does).
  • plot.py overlays several CSVs only on a thread axis. The cross-variant single-core plot therefore uses fresh one-configuration runs of each variant at its best block, and the BKP5 comparison a separate bs = 512 thread sweep.
  • Another user's rsync ran for much of the session and sometimes moved onto core 16. Rep spreads are reported with each experiment; the few large ones were single slow reps with the median unaffected.
  • The BKQ5 control build, the rectangular-tile gate builds and the schedule-insns test were scratch builds outside the project; their timings are quick checks (3 reps), not sweep CSVs.
  • Workbooks: each variant's plots/ directory; cross-variant plots in plots/. The step-by-step log, including every command, is record.md.
